Defenses — can we train the detector to resist?

Robust training (WP3 / D3). Three arms — plain training, PGD adversarial training, and TRADES — built under one matched budget, then scored by the same harness. One of the results is a genuine surprise; one of its readings is honestly still a question.

The attacks page showed two ways to break a frozen detector: destroy its accuracy (realistic degradations) or destroy its confidence ranking (the ACE family, at bit-identical accuracy). This page asks the natural next question — does adversarial training help? — and answers it with a controlled 3×6 table where every number below has been recomputed from the stored predictions with src/metrics.py (pure numpy, no GPU) and matches the run reports to the decimal.

The experimental discipline (why the comparison is fair)
All three arms start from one shared initialisation (phase1_init.pt, 7 clean epochs, fit-val clean 0.8697), run the same 12-epoch schedule on the same fit split, augmentation and batch order, and are scored by the unchanged WP1 harness on splits the training never saw. Model selection touches only a held-out slice of fit — never calib, never test. So a difference between two rows is a difference of method, not of data or budget. src/train.pyruns/train_wp3_matched.log

Why the budget is matched on optimizer steps, not wall clock

PGD adversarial training runs an inner attack loop (here 7 steps) on every batch before it takes a single weight update. So one PGD optimizer step costs k+1 = 8 forward/backward passes where plain training costs one. If you instead matched the three arms on wall-clock time, the plain arm would quietly take roughly 8× as many weight updates — and the table would be measuring who got more training, not which method is better. Matching optimizer steps (12 epochs each) removes that confound; the wall clock is reported alongside so the real cost stays visible rather than hidden.

The forensic-epsilon finding — 8/255 collapses the model

This is the result to put in front of two robustness researchers, because it contradicts a habit the whole field carries over from object recognition.

The first PGD-AT run used the standard ImageNet robustness budget, ε = 8/255. It did not just train worse — it collapsed to a constant output. The training loss decayed toward ln 2 = 0.6931 (uniform two-class output: 0.7102 by epoch 6, from 0.7647), and both clean and robust fit-val accuracy pinned at 0.4943, the majority-class rate. The run was killed at epoch 6. At a forensic budget the exact same code trains fine.

The mechanism — the ball is wider than the evidence
Deepfake evidence is small-amplitude, high-frequency residue: generator fingerprints, resampling traces, blending seams. An 8/255 L∞ ball (±8 grey levels on every pixel) is wide enough to erase that residue outright. Once it can, the robust-optimal classifier inside that ball genuinely is a constant — training is doing exactly what the objective asks, and the objective is wrong for this task. Robustness budgets transplanted from object recognition, where the signal is large-amplitude semantic structure, do not transfer to forensics. So the defenses train at forensic budgets (1–4/255) with a linear ε warm-up, and the collapse stays in the report as the finding it is. src/train.py docstringruns/train_wp3_eps8_255_collapsed.log

Three readings — and the third is a question, not a conclusion

Reading 1 — robust training works on the label axis
The PGD 2/255 attack (an accuracy attack — it flips predictions) takes the undefended model to 0.0000 accuracy. The AUROC(failure) is nan there because there are no correct predictions left to rank — that is the harness's empty-case guard firing, not a bug. Both defended models hold ~0.71 accuracy under the same attack. On the threat it was designed for, adversarial training does exactly what it promises.
Reading 2 — it costs clean accuracy, and that cost is real
Clean accuracy drops −2.6 pp for PGD-AT (0.8435 → 0.8177) and −5.6 pp for TRADES (0.8435 → 0.7877), and the undefended model keeps the best clean AURC of the three (52.5 vs 62.3 / 82.5). Robustness is not free; the trade-off belongs in the same sentence as the benefit. (As a bonus, some realistic robustness comes along anyway: JPEG q50 costs the standard model 12 pp of accuracy and costs PGD-AT essentially nothing, 0.8177 → 0.8169.)
Reading 3 — it ALSO protects confidence, which we did not expect
Against the ACE confidence attack — which leaves accuracy identical and attacks only the ranking — the undefended model's AUROC(failure) collapses to 0.1093 (a working confidence signal turned actively misleading), while the defended models hold at 0.6659 / 0.6541. Against the label-free over-confidence attack the defended models barely move (0.8006 → 0.7770; 0.7761 → 0.7789). The published hypothesis we set out to test (Ledda et al. — that adversarial training protects against under-confidence attacks but not over-confidence ones) does not reproduce in this setting.
…but reading 3 rests on one under-optimised epsilon — treat it as a question for the PIs
Three things keep this a question rather than a refutation, and they should be stated to the robustness PIs plainly:
  • The attack did not saturate its own budget on the defended arm. The over-confidence attack reached only ~64% of its L∞ budget (effective mean 2.565 of 4.0 grey levels) on the PGD-AT model while still touching every pixel (frac_perturbed = 1.0). An attack that moves every pixel but falls short of the ball is the textbook signature of vanishing-gradient masking — i.e. the defended model may merely be hiding its gradients from this particular attack, not truly resisting it.
  • The internal control cuts the other way. A 4×-larger budget (over-confidence at 4 grey levels) produces a smaller effect than ACE at ~1 grey level — a larger hammer doing less damage is what an under-optimised attack looks like.
  • Half the 2×2 is missing. The Ledda hypothesis is a differential (under- vs over-confidence). The under-confidence arm was never run — the code exists (src/attack_suite.py) but no such predictions were produced. Claiming a differential is contradicted with one of its two arms absent is the highest-risk sentence in the whole write-up.
So the honest framing is: "AT and TRADES resist this over-confidence attack, but the attack is a fixed 20-step single-restart descent whose effective ε on PGD-AT is only 64% of budget, and we never ran the under-confidence arm — so we treat this as a question, not a result." runs/CLAIM_AUDIT_2026-08-19.mdsrc/attack_suite.py

What this buys the actual system

The point of D3 is not the table — it is what the table does to a moderation pipeline. Carried through the selective-moderation policy (fitted per model at a 5% residual-risk SLA, then frozen), adversarial training takes residual risk under a label attack from 100% down to ~13% and under the ACE confidence attack from ~19% down to ~8% — at the price of the human-review bill roughly doubling, 27% → 50%. That is a staffing cost, not an accuracy cost, and it is the subject of the next page.

One-line summary
Robust training at a forensic budget works — on the label axis it is decisive, and it unexpectedly protects confidence too — but the confidence result rests on one under-optimised attack with its complementary arm unrun, so it is written up as an open question. And 8/255, the field's default, silently destroys the model.
