TrustFake — what was built, and what it means

A deepfake-detection moderation pipeline, stress-tested until it broke, so we know exactly how it breaks before anyone else finds out.

TrustFake is the TReC 2026 project on social-media image deepfake detection. The concrete task is deliberately small: given one image, decide real vs fake — where "fake" folds together fully-synthetic images and locally-tampered ones — so that a moderation pipeline can auto-allow the images it is confident are real, auto-remove the ones it is confident are fake, and send the uncertain middle to a human reviewer. Everything on this wiki is about one question a distributed-systems engineer already knows to ask of any automated decision system: when it is wrong, does it know? The answer here is unsettling, and it is the reason the whole project exists.

The one thesis, in one line: confidence can be hacked.
A detector produces two things — an answer (real / fake) and a confidence (how sure it is). The pipeline trusts the confidence to decide what to automate. We show these two are separable: an attacker can leave every answer untouched — accuracy identical to four decimals — while making the confidence systematically wrong, so the model is most sure precisely where it is mistaken. A system that abstains on its least-confident traffic then keeps the worst half and reviews the good half. Confidence is not a free by-product of accuracy; it is a second attack surface, and nothing in a standard accuracy benchmark would ever reveal it.

The headline result, stated plainly

Take the fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0 detector and the moderation policy tuned to keep automated errors under a 5% SLA on clean traffic. Now perturb each uploaded image by about one 8-bit grey level — the smallest change a JPEG or PNG file can even represent, invisible to a human, and a change that does not move accuracy at all. The policy does not fall back to caution. It gets more confident, automates more of the traffic, and is wrong on a third of it.

Read the two right-hand columns together.
Accuracy is bit-identical (0.8311 → 0.8311). Yet the residual risk — the error rate among the decisions the system made on its own, without a human — goes from under 5% to 33.06%, and it does so while automating more traffic (43.2% → 50.4% coverage). Roughly one in three automated decisions is now wrong, and the accuracy dashboard shows nothing. Source: runs/moderation_threatmodel.md. The mechanism, its two threat models (tensor-access vs file-only), and why the file-only version at 0.0005 harmlessly vanishes are on the attacks page.
The same number, from the other side.
On the detector itself, the attack drives failure-prediction AUROC — how well confidence sorts mistakes from correct answers — from 0.7703 to 0.0002. 0.5 would be a coin flip; below 0.5 means confidence now ranks the model's errors above its correct answers, almost perfectly inverted. Verified from runs/predictions/predictions_tf_efficientnet_b0_test_*.npz via src/metrics.py. This is a pure ranking failure: no image was misclassified that was not already misclassified — the attacker only rearranged the confidence order.
The honest counterweight — accuracy is defence-in-depth.
The stronger CLIP detector (clean accuracy 0.9289) suffers the same confidence collapse — failure-AUROC 0.8779 → 0.1812 — yet its moderation residual risk only moves 5.46% → 7.46% under the identical file-realisable attack. A low base error rate bounds the damage: the attacker can only reshuffle confidence around the few mistakes that exist. Accuracy you thought was about being right turns out to also be about being safe when your confidence is attacked. Source: runs/moderation_clip.md. Details on the moderation page.

Open — questions, not failures
ResNet parityThe confidence attack is measured on EfficientNet-B0 and CLIP. A second convolutional architecture (ResNet) has not been run, so architecture-generality of the effect is unconfirmed rather than established. The temperature bound, correctedTemperature scaling provably cannot reorder samples on the linear probes (max margin ≈ 12, AURC bit-identical for T ≥ 0.5). On the trained checkpoints (max margin ≈ 118) float64 softmax saturates to exactly 1.0 and collides neighbouring margins into tie blocks, so ordering is not T-invariant there — the guard is the operating-point count. This corrected claim is dissected on the epistemics page. The uncertainty (σ) seamThe PIs' decision diagram routes on a per-image uncertainty score σ — from MC-Dropout, ensembles or evidential heads that other subgroups build. The current npz contract carries logits only: there is no σ slot. Adding one and publishing it on Day 1 makes this harness the camp's integration point. Detailed on the pathways page. The confidence-defense question"Robust training also protects confidence" rests on one under-optimised epsilon (effective ε only ~64% of budget on PGD-AT — possible gradient masking) and the under-confidence attack arm was never run. It is a question to put to the PIs, not a settled result.
Also open from the audit: whether the detector rides the laundered compression shortcut (the geometry control proves it can't read aspect ratio, not that it ignores the resampling residue) — the settling experiment is named. See the dataset page.
