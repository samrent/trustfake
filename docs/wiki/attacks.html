Attacks — and the two ways a detector fails

A moderation detector can be broken in two independent ways: flip the label, or leave the label and move the confidence. They need different attacks, they show up in different numbers, and a system can be immune to one while wide open to the other.

This is the scientific spine of the whole project (deliverable D2, the attack package src/attack_suite.py). Everything else — the defenses, the moderation policy, the "how experiments lie" page — is downstream of getting this distinction right.

The one sentence to keep
Prediction-targeted attacks flip the label, so accuracy collapses. Confidence-targeted attacks keep the label and reorder the confidences, so accuracy is untouched and the ranking collapses instead. Report only one of the two numbers and the central claim becomes unfalsifiable — which is exactly why every table in this project prints accuracy beside AURC.

The taxonomy — three axes, not one list

Each attack in the registry (attack_suite.py, the ATTACKS dict) is filed on three axes. Family is what it targets. Direction is which way it pushes confidence. Uses labels is whether it needs ground truth — an attacker uploading to a real platform usually does not have the labels, so a label-free attack is the deployable one.

The direction axis (why over/under/both matters)

Confidence-targeted attacks come in three flavours, and the flavour decides which cost line in the moderation budget explodes (see the moderation page for the prices):

under-confidencePush confidence down on predictions that were correct. The model abstains on work it could have done. Cost: the review rate explodes — wasted moderator hours. Label-free. over-confidencePush confidence up on predictions that were wrong. The model auto-decides work it gets wrong. Cost: the residual risk explodes — wrong decisions shipped. This is the dangerous direction, and the one adversarial training is least likely to fix, because the entropy-minimising region sits in the interior of the decision cell, not on the boundary that AT hardens. Label-free, and label-preserving by construction. This is the WP3 hypothesis the attack exists to test. both (ACE)Push confidence down where correct and up where wrong, at once. That makes ACE the strongest single attack on a risk-coverage curve — and also why it needs ground-truth labels, which is its practical weakness in the wild.

Why "orthogonal" is the exact word
ACE moves accuracy by zero (0.8311 → 0.8311, argmax bit-identical) and sends AUROC(failure) to the floor (0.7703 → 0.0002). Ordinary compression does the opposite: downscaling drops accuracy to 0.68 while the ranking barely moves (0.7703 → 0.7013). The two axes are independent. An accuracy monitor is blind to ACE; a ranking-only monitor is blind to compression. You need both numbers or you are flying with one instrument covered.

The same contrast holds on a trained checkpoint, where the prediction-targeted family shows its full face. On the standard EfficientNet arm (runs/d3_comparative.md): PGD at 2/255 drives accuracy to 0.0000 (label flipped everywhere), while ACE uint8 on the same model leaves accuracy at 0.8435 and drops AUROC(failure) from 0.7945 to 0.1093. One attack empties the label; the other empties the ordering.

The sharpest point: what is actually realisable

ACE's tensor-access number (AUROC 0.7703 → 0.0002) is dramatic, but it is not the honest headline, because it needs something a real attacker may not have. The subtlety is in the grey levels.

Unquantised ACE perturbs each pixel by a fraction of one 8-bit step. Measured effective perturbations (runs/ace_sweep.log, cross-checked in runs/CLAIM_AUDIT_2026-08-19.md): mean per-sample L∞ of 0.102 / 0.261 / 0.447 grey levels at ε = 0.0005 / 0.002 / 0.005, with per-sample maxima 0.128 / 0.510 / 1.275. A saved image file can only hold integer grey levels (k/255), and round-to-nearest annihilates any change below half a grey level (0.5/255 = 0.00196; the step itself is 1/255 = 0.00392). So:

Unquantised ACEdescribes an attacker with post-decode tensor access — someone already inside the pipeline, after the image has been decoded to floats. ACE uint8snaps every perturbed pixel back onto k/255 — the file-realisable attacker who can only upload a PNG. This is the moderation threat model that actually matters.

The honest headline
At ε = 0.0005 the attack vanishes on the file lattice — that is a genuine control, not a weak result. At ε = 0.002 and 0.005, exactly 3,889 of 15,316 images (25.4%) retain a full one-grey-level change, and that quarter of the traffic is enough to send AUROC(failure) to 0.0471. The headline is therefore not "ACE destroys ranking" (that needs tensor access) but "a quarter of uploads, perturbed by one grey level, is enough." Under the frozen 5%-SLA moderation policy this same one-grey-level attack moves residual risk from 4.65% to 33.06% and pushes coverage up from 43.2% to 50.4% — the pipeline auto-decides more while getting more of it wrong. Details on the moderation page.

The ε = 0.002 and ε = 0.005 rows are identical, which looks like a copy-paste bug but is a mechanism: round-to-nearest maps both halving ladders (0.51, 0.26, … and 1.28, 0.64, 0.32, …) onto the same two-point set {one grey level, nothing}, so both attacks explore the same lattice.

The discipline this forces
Tensor-access numbers must never be quoted as file-upload numbers — a saved file is a different input, not because the perturbation is "erased" but because it lives on a different grid. Every attacked row in this project is labelled with which threat model it belongs to. The unquantised 0.0002 and the file-realisable 0.0471 are both true; they answer different questions.

The same attack against the stronger detector

The CLIP ViT-L/14 probe (n = 4,000) is hit by the identical file-realisable ACE: accuracy stays at 0.9323 and AUROC(failure) falls from 0.8779 to 0.1812 (0.0102 under tensor access). But because CLIP's clean error rate is so low, its residual risk under the moderation policy only moves from 5.46% to 7.46% — the small error rate bounds the damage. That is the one place where plain accuracy earns its keep: it is defence-in-depth against a ranking attack. See moderation and defenses.

The AutoAttack-on-binary gotcha

AutoAttack is the standard "did you actually test robustness or fool yourself" ensemble. It does not run out of the box on a two-class detector, and the way it fails is a trap.

apgd-t needs four logits; a detector has two
The targeted component apgd-t reads x_sorted[:,-3] and [:,-4] to build its targeted DLR loss, so it needs ≥ 4 logits and raises IndexError: index -3 is out of bounds for dimension 1 with size 2 on a binary head. The package warns for ≤ 2 classes and then runs anyway. Worse: with version='standard' the crash only fires once points survive the earlier apgd-ce stage — so it appears to work against a weak detector and dies against a robust one, i.e. it breaks precisely when the defense (WP3) succeeds. Forcing n_classes=3 on a 2-logit model does not help.

The mechanism (DLR needing four logits) is derivable and certain. The safe composition used here is apgd-ce + fab-t (with n_target_classes=1) + square, with Square query-bounded because it is by far the slowest piece (~2.8 img/s). One honest caveat: the exact IndexError is documented in the code from a development run, and under the no-GPU audit constraint it was not re-executed — it is marked as a code-recorded observation, not an independently re-run artifact.

AutoAttack(model, norm="Linf", eps=eps, version="custom",
           attacks_to_run=["apgd-ce", "fab-t", "square"])
aa.fab.n_target_classes = 1     # default reaches for logits that do not exist
aa.square.n_queries    = 500    # Square is the slow component; bound it

What to take away

Two failure modeslabel-flip (accuracy) and confidence-reorder (ranking) are independent; measure both, always. Direction is a costover-confidence buys residual risk, under-confidence buys review rate; they are not symmetric. Label-free is deployableACE is strongest but needs labels; the over/under-confidence attacks need none. Realisable ≠ tensorthe honest number is the file-lattice one; the one-grey-level attack on a quarter of traffic is the headline. Binary breaks toolingAutoAttack silently mis-runs on 2 logits; the composition matters.
