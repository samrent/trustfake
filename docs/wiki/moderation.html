Moderation — the layer where everything joins

A detector outputs a number. A moderation system has to turn that number into an action, on a schedule, at a cost. WP4 is that layer, and it is where the abstract findings of the attacks and defenses pages become operational: how many deepfakes ship, how many real users get flagged, how many moderators you have to pay.

Think of it as an admission controller in front of a queue. Every image gets one of three verdicts. Two of them are automatic; the third is a request for a human.

ALLOWauto-accept as real — no human sees it REVIEWescalate to a human moderator — the expensive path FLAGauto-flag as fake — no human sees it

The code (src/moderation.py) is deliberately small. The whole policy is two thresholds on p(fake), the model's estimated probability that the image is fake:

p_fake <  t_low            -> ALLOW
p_fake >  t_high           -> FLAG
t_low <= p_fake <= t_high  -> REVIEW   (the uncertainty band)

Why two thresholds, and why on the risk axis

A distributed-systems instinct is to threshold on confidence — one knob, escalate anything below it. That is exactly the symmetric special case t_low = 1 − tau, t_high = tau, so nothing is lost by generalising. But moderation costs are not symmetric. Auto-allowing a deepfake ships harm. Auto-flagging a real photo annoys a user. Sending either to review costs a moderator's minute. Three different prices — two thresholds let you set them independently; one confidence knob silently asserts they are equal.

The one rule that makes the numbers honest
The thresholds are fitted once, on the clean calibration split, and then frozen. They are applied unchanged to the clean test set and to every attacked condition. Re-fitting on attacked data would be an oracle policy — it assumes the moderator already knows an attack is underway, which is the exact assumption the attack defeats. Freezing is what lets the tables below show a real collapse instead of a laundered one.

Four deployment indicators carry the story. Two are the obvious cost/coverage pair; two more split the errors by who they hurt.

coveragefraction auto-decided (1 − review rate) — the automation you actually got residual_riskerror rate among the auto-decided — what actually ships wrong missed_fake_ratefakes auto-ALLOWED, as a fraction of all fakes — the harm false_flag_ratereals auto-FLAGGED, as a fraction of all reals — the annoyance

One design detail worth stealing: if a condition auto-decides almost nothing, its residual risk is reported as NaN, not 0.0. A system that refuses to decide anything has no residual risk to measure, and printing 0.0 would make total refusal to work look like perfect safety — the single most flattering misreading of a collapsed system. The SLA fitter enforces the same honesty: if no threshold pair meets the target, the policy degrades to review-everything rather than quietly returning the least-bad infeasible pair.

The headline: the realisable attack at unchanged accuracy

The attacks page establishes that the file-realisable confidence attack (ace_uint8, a perturbation that survives being written back to an 8-bit PNG) leaves accuracy bit-identical while corrupting the model's ranking. Read the accuracy column, then the risk column
Accuracy is 0.8311 in every row of the attack — the model is exactly as often right about the label. Yet at eps0.002 the residual risk of the auto-decided pool goes from 4.65% to 33.06% — a third of everything that ships without a human is wrong — and the harm half of that (fakes auto-allowed) rises from 2.34% to 17.01%. The policy has no idea. It is reading a confidence signal that the attacker owns.

The counter-intuitive column is coverage. It goes up under the realisable attack, 43.2% → 50.4%. The attack inflates the model's confidence, so it pushes borderline items out of the review band and into auto-decisions — and those newly-automated items are the wrong ones. The human safety net doesn't just fail to grow with the threat; it shrinks exactly when it is most needed. (The unquantised variant does the opposite — it scatters p(fake) so violently that most items fall into review, coverage collapses to 17.1%, and the few that escape are almost all wrong. That version is the worst case, but it needs post-decode tensor access, not a file. See the attacks page for the threat-model split.)

For contrast, the realistic corruptions (JPEG re-compression, downscaling) attack the other axis — they cost accuracy, not ranking — so the same policy degrades gracefully: jpeg_q50 lands at 7.45% residual risk, downscale_0.5 at 22.46% but driven by false-flags (annoyance), not missed fakes. runs/moderation_report.json. Two orthogonal failure modes; the epistemics page is about not confusing them.

The defense payoff — a staffing cost, not an accuracy cost

Do the adversarially-trained checkpoints from the defenses page buy anything here, at the moderation layer? Yes — and the shape of the payoff is the interesting part. Under real PGD attack: 100% → 13% residual risk
The standard checkpoint collapses to 100% residual risk under PGD 2/255 — its accuracy goes to zero, it emits a constant output, everything it auto-decides is wrong. The adversarially-trained arms hold the auto-decided pool to 12.97% (PGD-AT) and 13.51% (TRADES). That is the defense paying off exactly where D3 said it would.

But read the last two rows. The price of that robustness is not accuracy — it is the review rate on clean traffic, which jumps from 27.1% to ~50%. The robust models are less confident, so more images fall into the human band even when nothing is wrong. In an ops budget that is a staffing line: roughly double the moderators for the same clean throughput. TRADES additionally pays ~5.6 points of clean accuracy (0.8435 → 0.7877). Whether that trade is worth it is a business decision the harness refuses to make for you — it just prints both columns.

Accuracy is defence-in-depth — the CLIP finding

The most useful single result in WP4 is a comparison the moderation layer makes visible and the raw attack metrics hide. Run the same file-realisable confidence attack against the CLIP ViT-L/14 probe (a more accurate base detector) and against EfficientNet-B0. The attack succeeds identically at the ranking level — it destroys CLIP's failure-detection just as thoroughly. But the thing that ships is bounded very differently. Sources: runs/moderation_clip.json, runs/condition_table_clip.md (n = 4000 sample).

CLIP conditionacccoverage review%resid. riskAUROC(fail)
clean0.932395.2%4.8% 5.46%0.8779
ace_uint8 eps0.0050.932390.8% 9.2%7.46%0.1812

AUROC(fail) — the model's ability to know when it is wrong — collapses from 0.8779 to 0.1812 (worse than a coin flip; the confidence signal is inverted). By that metric CLIP is just as broken as EfficientNet. Yet the residual risk of its frozen policy moves only 5.46% → 7.46%, where EfficientNet went to 33%.

Why the same attack ships one-fifth the harm
Residual risk is an error rate, and an error rate is bounded by how often the model is wrong at all. CLIP's clean error rate is ~7%, so however cleverly the attacker reshuffles confidence, it cannot make the auto-decided pool much worse than the base error rate — there simply are not enough mistakes to promote. EfficientNet's ~17% error rate leaves far more room. The lesson for a system designer: a more accurate base model is not just more accurate — it is a second, independent layer of defense against the confidence attack. The two failure modes on the epistemics page — attacks on ranking vs attacks on accuracy — are why this works: hardening the accuracy axis caps the damage the ranking attack can do. (Note the honest caveat: CLIP still pays coverage, 95.2% → 90.8%, so more images go to humans; the attack is not free, it is just contained.)

The demonstrator — and its refusal to oversell itself

src/demo.py is the thing you can put on a screen on 4 Sep: drop an image, get the three-zone verdict live. It is stdlib-only (http.server, no Flask) on purpose — a demonstrator that depends on a web framework is the thing that breaks when the camp wifi does. It loads the detector once, runs on CPU if the 3090 is busy holding a dictation model, and serves a single self-contained page on 127.0.0.1:8471.

sample buttonsreal / fully-synthetic / tampered, drawn only from the TEST split — a demo over training images would be a demo of memorisation (demo.log: real=7658, synthetic=3829, tampered=3829) ground-truth reveala sample carries its true label in an X-Truth header; the page shows whether the detector was right or wrong, not just its verdict policy is loaded, not computedthresholds come straight from moderation_report.json (frozen on clean calib). A demo that re-derived its own thresholds would be a different system from the one the report measures

The most important thing the demo does is on the page itself, in a card titled "What this is not." It states, unprompted, that this is a research prototype over a held-out slice of the SID-Set validation split (the official test split is withheld by the dataset authors), that it is a frozen backbone with a linear head measured on one dataset, and that its confidence is known to be attackable — quoting its own headline number: a perturbation that leaves accuracy bit-identical drives the residual risk of this very policy from 4.7% to 98.1%. The demonstrator refuses to claim deployability. That refusal is the deliverable as much as the verdict box is.

Honest limits — what this layer does not prove

Read these as plainly as the results
  • Validation slice, not the real test set. Every number here is on a held-out slice of the SID-Set validation split; the official test split is withheld. Absolute rates would move on the real test set.
  • The SLA is a choice, not a fact. 5% residual risk means one in twenty auto-decisions is wrong. A real platform sets that from the cost of a wrong decision and the size of its moderation team. It is written into every artifact so a reader can disagree explicitly rather than inherit it silently.
  • "AT protects confidence" rests on one under-optimised epsilon. The overconfidence attack reached only ~64% of its budget against PGD-AT — possible gradient masking, not proven robustness — and the under-confidence arm of that attack was never run. So the defense payoff table is a question for the PIs, not a settled refutation of the threat.
  • The base detectors ride a laundered shortcut we have not ruled out. The dataset page shows the geometry control proves the model can't read aspect ratio — it does not prove it isn't riding the compression shortcut. Every residual-risk number inherits that open question.
